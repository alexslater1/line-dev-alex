Introduction
Modern software systems have become increasingly more complex due to the widespread adoption of
distributed, multi-tier architectures. Client-server and microservice-based systems are commonly
used to achieve scalability and flexibility; however, they also introduce significant performance
analysis challenges. Synchronous request–reply interactions, limited server concurrency and shared
hardware resources can lead to blocking, contention and non-linear behaviour, making it difficult
to predict system performance.
A well-established approach to understanding such systems is an analytical performance modelling technique known as queueing network models. A queueing network models a system as a
collection of interconnected service stations, each with an associated queue of jobs awaiting service. Jobs circulate through the network and incur both queueing time, representing time spent
waiting for service, and service time, representing time spent being processed at a station. By
analysing these models, it is possible to derive important performance metrics such as average
response time, throughput, queue length and resource utilisation [1]. These metrics can be used
to guide architectural decisions and identify bottlenecks early in the design process of computer
systems.
Queueing network models have been widely applied within computer systems research, including the analysis of processors and I/O devices, transaction processing systems and distributed
applications [1]. Their importance has increased as modern systems demand high throughput and
low latency, allowing performance considerations to be addressed before implementation. Compared to techniques such as measurement or simulation, analytical models allow rapid exploration
of system designs at a relatively low cost, making them particularly valuable during the initial
stages of development [2].
In this project, we focus on the application of queueing models on distributed server systems.
Classical queueing network models, together with solution techniques such as Mean Value Analysis, rely on restrictive assumptions, including product-form behaviour and the lack of blocking [1].
Whilst these assumptions allow for efficient solutions, they are frequently violated in client–server
systems. For example, synchronous remote procedure calls introduce simultaneous resource possession, where both client and server resources are held during a call, invalidating the independence
assumptions required by classical queueing network solvers [2].
To address these limitations, Layered Queueing Networks (LQNs) were introduced as an extension of classical queueing models, specifically designed for client–server architectures. LQNs
represent software entities, such as tasks and entries, with their limited concurrency and synchronous interactions, while also modelling hardware resources, such as processors. By structuring
the model into layers and representing synchronous calls as rendezvous interactions, LQNs are able
to capture blocking, nested service demands and contention effects that arise naturally in multitier systems [3, 2]. This makes LQNs well-suited to modelling and analysing modern distributed
software architectures.
However, the increased modelling power of LQNs comes at the cost of increased computational
and implementation complexity. LQN solvers rely on decomposition techniques, surrogate delays
and approximate variants of MVA combined with fixed-point iteration [1, 2]. These techniques
have been used in LQNS and other advanced solver tools, which support a wide range of modelling
constructs such as interlocking, multiservers and complex activity graphs [2]. While this generality enables the analysis of realistic systems, it also results in large and complex codebases that
4

are difficult to understand, modify, or extend and makes scalability harder through less efficient
algorithms.
This project aims to explore the trade-off between modelling power and solver complexity
by implementing a simplified LQN solver inspired by LQNS. Rather than reproducing the full
functionality of existing solvers, the project focuses on a restricted but representative subset of
LQN features. The objective is to develop a solver that is easier to understand and extend, and
solves LQNs more efficiently, while retaining the essential characteristics that distinguish LQNs
from classical queueing networks.

Queueing Network Modelling
Queueing networks provide a formal abstraction for evaluating performance in computer systems,
where jobs (or customers) contend for shared system resources. These networks enable analysts
to derive key metrics, such as utilisation, throughput and response time, without requiring fully
built systems. This section introduces the structure and principles underlying queueing models,
including their components, performance laws, and outputs, with a focus on separable product-form
models as the basis for more advanced approaches like layered queueing networks.

2.1

Basics

A queueing network model represents a system using two primary entities: customers, which model
users or transactions; and service centres, which model resources such as CPUs, disks, or servers.
These components can take different forms, take close correspondence to the attributes of computer
systems, allowing them to naturally be modelled.

2.1.1

Customers

The behaviour of customers can be characterised into three different types, enabling the modelling
of systems of various forms, as specified by the workload intensities shown below [1]:
• A transaction workload is used to represent independent requests arriving from the outside
world, behaviour typical of API calls and database queries. This arrival-driven workload is
specified by the arrival rate, λ, at which jobs arrive into the system. Customers are serviced
and then leave the model on completion, causing a population that varies over time.
• A batch workload represents a fixed number of jobs inside the system. This is typical of
analytical jobs, and it works under the assumption that when a job finishes and leaves the
system, it is immediately replaced by another job from the same workload. This ensures
constant population size, specified by parameter N , relating to the average population size
of the true workload.
• A terminal workload models jobs that pause between requests, typical of timesharing systems
and interactive users. Again, the population is fixed by parameter N , however instead of
exiting the system and being replaced immediately, the user thinks for a specified time, Z,
before submitting the next request.
A model that contains only transaction workloads is known as an open model, as there is an infinite
stream of incoming transactions causing varied population size, whilst a model that contains only
batch and terminal workloads is a closed model as it consists of a fixed number of customers that
re-circulate. A model that consists of both open and closed classes is known as a mixed model. [1]
For the purpose of this project, we will only consider closed models, as the techniques used to
solve open and closed models differ.

2.1.2

Service Centres

Service Centres represent any system resource that provides service to customers, and can be of
two different types [1]:
• Queuing centres are service centres where customers compete for a limited resource, in which
they may experience a wait time whilst they are in the queue before the resource becomes
available. They then spend the necessary service time at the resource. This can be used to
model computing resources such as CPU or Disk. In a single-class model, it is not necessary
to establish a scheduling discipline for the queue, however when we introduce multiple classes
of customers, this may become required.
• Delay centres are service centres where customers do not compete, instead they are each
treated as having their own servers. Therefore, there is no queue, resulting in no wait
time, just the service time specified. This can be used to model user think time or fixed
communication delays.

Vk Sk where Vk is the number of visits made to service centre k and Sk is the service requirement

The Flow Balance Assumption

It may be useful to assume that the systems we are looking at satisfy the assumption that the
number of arrivals is equal to the number of completions. Equivalently, the arrival rate to the
system equals the throughput of the system. A = C, therefore λ = X. This can be used in
conjunction with Little’s Law and the Forced Flow Law to calculate device utilisations for systems
with terminal workload intensities. [1]

2.3

Outputs

Once a queueing model is defined and evaluated, we are able to obtain a list of outputs useful for
performance analysis. As the inputs to the model are described as averages over time, so will the
outputs. We can show that the value of an output relates to a specific input through notation such
as X(N ), where X is the throughput for a batch or terminal class with population N . We now
discuss key performance measures that will help us analyse our system. [1]

2.3.1

Utilisation

Denoted as Uk , the utilisation of centre k is defined as the proportion of time device k is busy or,
for a delay centre, the average number of customers in service there. We can use the utilisation
law, UK = Xk Sk , to calculate this for each device. [1]

Throughput

The throughput at centre k, Xk , is the rate of job completions. Interestingly, the throughput of

Queue Length

The average queue length at centre k, denoted by Qk , includes both customers being served and
customers waiting to be served. Therefore the number of customers waiting can be calculated from
Qk − Uk . We let Q denote the average number of customers in the system. Therefore, for a batch
class Q = N , the fixed population within the subsystem, and a terminal class Q = N − XZ, the
total population, subtracting those who are thinking. [1]

Mean Value Analysis
Having established the structure of queueing network models, their inputs, and the fundamental
performance laws, we now need to establish our solution technique. Mean Value Analysis (MVA)
provides an efficient analytic technique for solving queueing networks by iteratively computing
mean performance measures without requiring explicit enumeration of the underlying Markov state
space. By exploiting product-form properties and conservation laws, MVA enables the evaluation
of systems with multiple service centres and customer classes at a computational cost much less
than that of simulation or experimentation. As a result, MVA has become a widely used solution
techniques for performance analysis of computer systems. As previously mentioned, we will only
be looking at solving closed models in this project. It is possible to solve open models in a similar
fashion.

Extended Queueing Networks
Classical queueing networks and Mean Value Analysis provide efficient solution techniques under product-form assumptions; however, these assumptions are frequently violated in distributed
client–server systems. We must assess the limitations of this existing approach and introduce the
extensions that motivate Layered Queueing Networks.

4.1

Assumptions of Product-Form Queueing Networks

While product-form queueing networks enable efficient computation of performance measures, as
demonstrated in the previous chapters, they rely on an underlying set of assumptions [2]:
• Service centres are of one of these specific types:
– A single server with a first-come-first-served queueing discipline, where the time a job
needs at the server is exponentially distributed. The mean service times for all customers
must be identical.
– A single server with a last-come-first-served, random or processor-sharing discipline.
Service time distributions can be general (do not require the memoryless property), and
different customers can have different service times.
– A load-dependent first-come-first-served service centre, where the service rate is dependent on the number of customers at the station only.
• The population of a closed product form network is fixed
• Customer routing is independent of the state of the network
Whilst these assumptions may seem restrictive, there are many models where they are violated,
yet produce sufficiently accurate results. However, there are some cases in which product-form
queueing networks cannot be used directly [2]:
• Systems with simultaneous resource possession: The service rate at one centre may
be dependent on other centres in the network.
• Systems with fork-join behaviour: Forking generates new customers, whilst joining eliminates them, violating the fixed customer population and routing homogeneity assumptions.
Simultaneous resource possession is common in computer systems in places where there is
limited multi-programming, lock contention or remote procedure calls. If these effects are ignored,
we could overestimate the throughput of a model, as we are not accounting for the time required
to acquire the resources. [2]
This is where we consider the method of surrogates, accounting for the time needed to acquire
simultaneously held resources by splitting the input model into submodels and iterating between
them. This works as each submodels contains the explicit representation of one of the resources,
and a delay centre representing the other. The waiting time for one of the resources is therefore
solved and then used as the delay centre input in the other model. This process repeats, iterating
between models until convergence. [2]
16

4.2

Client-Server Models

To address the limitations of classical queueing networks, we now consider the adaptations that are
tailored towards client-server architecture. These approaches all use hierarchical decomposition and
the method of surrogates, breaking multi-tier client-server architecture into two-layer systems that
are solved iteratively. Whilst the overall approach is similar, they take different implementation
forms. [2]

4.2.1

Stochastic Rendezvous Network Model

The Stochastic Rendezvous Network Model consists of an acyclic graph of clients and servers
(referred to as tasks), used to model users and both hardware and software components, connected
through remote procedure calls (where the clients are blocked until the server responds). The input
model is broken down into a set of submodels that each consist of one server, the set of clients
that call it, and surrogate delays for the other calls that the clients make. The clients are treated
as unique customers, with a population based on the number of instances of the task. The model
is then solved by applying one-step MVA to each of the submodels, adjusting the surrogate delays
in the other submodels, and iterating until convergence. This allows the modelling of requests
with rendezvous semantics, in which clients are blocked until servers reply, directly modelling
synchronous remote procedure calls and nested service dependencies. [2]

4.2.2

Method of Layers

The Method of Layers explores hierarchical decomposition as a solution strategy for client–server
systems, partitioning the system into a set of two-level MVA submodels. These are constructed
by splitting the input model into a hardware contention submodel, containing all tasks as clients
and devices as servers, and a software contention submodel, which, only considering tasks, sorts
the model into layers and constructs submodel n by using all tasks on level n as clients, and all
tasks at level n + 1 as servers. Surrogate delays are also introduced into the software contention
submodels after servers that make a call to another task in the input model. Surrogate delays are
introduced into the hardware contention submodel, with one for each task. These submodels are
solved by iterating from submodel 1 to N − 1 until convergence. These measures are then used as
the think and service times in the hardware model, which is solved, and the measures put back into
the software submodels. This process repeats until convergence of both. This approach provides
a scalable mechanism for analysing systems with synchronous interactions, where the surrogate
delays act as approximates for blocking effects without requiring joint state modelling. [2]

Solving Layered Queueing Networks
We now explore how LQNs are solved in practice, focusing on the algorithmic principles used by the
Layered Queueing Network Solver (LQNS). LQNS provides a scalable way to analyse client–server
systems that violate the independence assumptions required by classical product-form queueing
networks. It does so by treating the layered model as a collection of interacting queueing networks,
each capturing contention at a particular portion of the call hierarchy, and then enforcing consistency between them via fixed-point iteration in waiting times. The practical consequence is that
LQNS can compute the same kinds of outputs as classical queueing solvers, while modelling nested
synchronous calls and resource contention across both software and hardware layers. We will take
inspiration from this approach when implementing our LQN solver.

5.1

Layered Queueing Network Basics

LQNS is based upon the Stochastic Rendezvous Network Model we explored earlier. The inputs
to the model are: tasks, which represent hardware and software objects (service centres); entries,
which define their service demands at each of the tasks (customers); and phases, denoting the
different stages of service within entries, allowing situations where remote procedure calls can
respond before service is fully completed. [2]

5.1.1

Tasks

Tasks in the Stochastic Rendezvous Network Model can be classified in three ways [2]:
• Pure Clients - These are service centres that only send messages. They can be used to model
input sources such as users, as they cycle continuously.
• Pure Servers - These are service centres that only receive requests, used to model hardware
servers such as processors or disks.
• Active Servers - These are service centres that both accept and produce requests. These are
typically software processes.
Tasks communicate with each other using the send-receive-reply paradigm, where the client is
blocked until it receives a response from the server. Task service times are broken down into phases,
with the task responding to the client at the end of phase one, and then executing subsequent
autonomous phases. Messages are passed to the server on a first-come-first-served basis, but the
server can differentiate its actions based on the entry, analogous to classes in conventional queueing
networks. [2]

centres, where the queueing discipline is derived from the task properties, in each submodel.

summing the service times for the entries it calls plus its own. We can then calculate the type

minimum service time. [2]
The number of customers in the submodel is derived based on the number of potentially active
instances of a task. Single-threaded FCFS fixed-rate tasks have one customer. Multiserver clients
have a population equal to the minimum of the number of servers and the number of clients that
call the multiserver. Infinite servers have a number of customers equal to the number of clients
that call the task. [2]

Evaluation Plan
Evaluation is a critical component of this project, as it determines whether the proposed solver
meets its objectives and whether the design choices we have made are justified. The evaluation
focuses on three aspects: functional correctness, performance and scalability, and the trade-offs
introduced by adopting a simplified layered queueing network solver.

7.1

Functional Correctness

The first stage of evaluation assesses the functional correctness of the solver. This involves verifying
that the solver produces results that are consistent with known solutions for baseline models. Since
the solver does not implement the full feature set of existing tools such as LQNS, differences are
expected for more complex models. Consequently, this evaluation focuses primarily on fundamental
scenarios that fall within the scope of our model’s feature set. Where appropriate, results will be
compared against those produced by LINE or LQNS to verify that the solver exhibits similar
qualitative trends, such as bottleneck identification and saturation behaviour.

7.2

Performance

The second stage of evaluation examines the performance of the solver. This includes assessing how
the solver scales with increasing model size, such as the number of tasks, the depth of layering, and
client population sizes. Performance will be measured primarily in terms of runtime and the number
of iterations required to reach convergence. While absolute runtime comparisons with LQNS must
be interpreted carefully due to differences in implementation language and optimisations made,
comparisons with LINE are more appropriate. In addition, the number of iterations provides an
implementation-independent metric that reflects the efficiency of the underlying algorithms rather
than low-level optimisations.

7.3

Trade-Offs

The final stage of evaluation involves a discussion of the trade-offs associated with adopting a
simplified layered queueing network solver. This includes identifying which modelling features are
supported and which are intentionally omitted, together with the rationale behind these design
decisions. The impact of these choices on accuracy, scalability, and implementation complexity
will be analysed. We can use this to discuss how reductions in these algorithms can lead to
simpler and potentially faster analysis, while also highlighting the limitations introduced by these
simplifications.
